Mantras, manifestos, concepts, principles — four different jobs

Noticing the four words being used interchangeably on my own sites, and not being able to say quickly what separates them.

A concept names something. It answers "what is this?" and its test is recognition: once you have the word, you start seeing the thing. Shift-left is a concept. It does not tell you to do anything; it gives a name to moving the check earlier, and the name is what lets you argue about it. A concept can be wrong, and can be replaced by a better name for the same thing. A principle tells you what to do when you have to choose. It answers "which way?" and its test is that it can be violated — if no realistic decision could contradict it, it is not a principle but a pleasantry. "Structure beats magic" is a principle: it rules out the clever one-off in favour of the boring repeatable thing, and it costs you something every time you follow it. Anything that only sounds nice fails this test. A mantra is a principle worn smooth by repetition. It answers "what do I tell myself?" and its test is that it works under pressure, when there is no time to reason. Mantras are shorter than principles and less precise on purpose — the precision is what you lose in exchange for being able to recall it at the moment of choosing. "Take the time" is a mantra. It is not an argument, it is a handhold. A manifesto is a public commitment. It answers "what do I stand for, in front of others?" and its test is that someone can hold you to it. It collects principles and mantras and puts them where they can be checked, which is the whole point and the reason it is the hardest of the four to write honestly. The Holstee poster works because every line is something you could be caught not doing. The order runs one way: concepts name, principles decide, mantras carry, manifestos commit. You cannot write a manifesto out of concepts alone — you get a glossary. And a mantra without a principle behind it is a slogan, which is why most of them do not survive contact with a hard week.

The pictures went to mosaic, and then to bright

A photograph of a ceramic wall mosaic — a bridge, a church spire, a banana tree, a plain yellow sun — handed over with three words: "I like this."

The site's illustrations began as flat saturated colour, built from five of my own holiday photographs: a hand-painted cafe table, sun-bleached houses with washing on the balconies, stacked coloured chairs, beach huts striped at dusk, a Sri Lankan elephant covered in pattern. That version was right about the taste and wrong about the technique. Flat colour is a poster. It states a mood; it does not make one. A mosaic does something flat colour cannot. Its colour is not applied, it is assembled — a sky is not one blue but thirty, with white and yellow shards scattered through it, and the grout between them shows that a person laid every piece. That is the same rule the photographs were already teaching: lived-in, not styled. Now it is in the technique rather than the subject matter. Two corrections followed, both from looking rather than reasoning. The first attempt came back as uniform rounded squares in rows — a tiled bathroom wall, not a broken plate reassembled. The second was richly coloured but tinted, everything filtered through orange and rust, and earthy is precisely what glazed ceramic is not. Glaze is glassy and cool-bright: sky blue, grass green, buttercup yellow, and clean white tiles scattered through, because white is what makes the colours beside it read as bright. The flat version is kept rather than overwritten. A rejected style is evidence of a decision, and evidence is cheap to store.

Not a research sabbatical, then not an ordinary one either

Looking up what kinds of sabbatical exist, and finding a typology with a gap in it — achiever, explorer, burned-out (HBR, 2023), plus the by-purpose split into research, professional development and personal growth.

The year started under a borrowed label. Research sabbatical was a real category and the closest fit on offer, so the site said "Not a research sabbatical" — positioning itself inside a framework by arguing with one of its entries. That held until the breakthrough wording arrived and made the borrowing visible: this year is not a variant of an existing type, it is one the typology does not have. Achiever is too narrow, explorer too aimless, burned-out plainly wrong. The homepage now says "Not an ordinary sabbatical", which trades precision for reach — most readers have never met the categories, but everyone has a picture of a year off, and that picture is what the year argues with. The sharper claim is still sitting unused: three types exist and none of them fit.
